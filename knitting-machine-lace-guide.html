Machine lace explained: eyelets, yarn transfers & punchcards

Every punchcard lace pattern — feather and fan, shell, leaf, bobble lace — is built from two actions: making a hole and moving a loop sideways. On a hand-knit piece you do both with needles. On a machine, one of them is a mechanical constraint and the other is a scheduling problem, and that is why machine lace behaves so differently from hand lace.

1. Why a hole is harder than it looks

An eyelet is a yarnover: yarn laid over a needle without a stitch under it, so the next row loops up around it and opens a hole. In hand knitting you make one by simply putting the yarn forward.

A knitting machine cannot do that. Needles only receive yarn when they are in working position and there is a loop on them to be cleared, or when a carriage wraps an empty needle deliberately. So machine eyelets are manufactured in two steps:

  1. Empty the needle. Transfer its loop onto a neighbour. The needle is now out of work.
  2. Knit past it. On the next plain pass the empty needle rises and catches the yarn, which forms a new, oversized loop — the hole, once it settles.

That is why an eyelet and a transfer are not duplicate tools. The transfer is the mechanism; the eyelet is the result. In KNITCAT's chart, the ○ Eyelet cell states the intent (“a hole here”) and the decompiler pays for it with a one-needle transfer — unless you have already drawn an adjacent ↖/↗ transfer or a ∧ decrease to foot the bill, in which case it uses yours. Same hole, one fewer pass.

Keeping the stitch count honest

A transfer removes a needle from work; a yarnover adds a loop back. A repeat that only transfers shrinks; one that only makes holes grows. Traditional machine lace pairs them: one yarnover per single transfer, two per centred double decrease, and the width comes out unchanged. Count them across your repeat — the arithmetic is the design.

2. The directional transfer rule

A lace carriage's transfer cam is a shaped metal blade. Pushed left → right, it sweeps each selected loop to the needle on its right. Pushed right → left, it moves loops left. It cannot choose.

So the pattern's geometry is tied to carriage travel:

This is why a lace motif's left and right halves are rarely symmetrical on the card even when they look symmetrical in the fabric: the machine can only obey one direction per pass.

3. Transfer collisions and why passes multiply

Three physical impossibilities turn “one row of the chart” into several carriage passes:

  • Read-write race — needle i hands its loop to i+1 at the same moment i+1 hands its loop away. The cam reads a needle mid-vacation; a loop drops.
  • Cascading transfers — i → i+1 and i+1 → i+2 in one pass: order matters, and the cams cannot enforce it.
  • Converging transfers — two loops landing on one needle in the same pass (what a decrease wants). The second arrival pushes the first off; that is a dropped stitch, not a decrease.

The fix is scheduling, not design compromise: split the conflicting operations into separate passes and alternate the carriage direction between them. Because the carriage ends where it finished, every pass flips which transfers are legal next — and that is what produces the pass counts people find shocking on lace cards. KNITCAT models this as a directed acyclic graph and reports the resulting carriage pass schedule row by row, with the punch holes for each pass.

4. Plain anchor rows, and Brother vs Silver Reed

Brother (separated carriages)

The Brother LC-5, LC-7 and LC-8 / LC-2 lace carriages select and transfer but carry no yarn. After the lace sequence, the main K-carriage must knit — normally two plain rows — to feed yarn to the vacated needles and anchor the eyelets. Fashion lace patterns are therefore always “lace passes, then plain rows”, and the card has to include the blank rows.

Silver Reed / Studio / Singer (combined carriage)

The Silver Reed LC-1 / LC-5 / LC-9 lace carriages transfer and knit in the same pass, so fewer blank rows are needed and the same chart compiles to a shorter card. Gauge geometry is nearly identical (4.5 mm), so the two systems' cards look interchangeable and are not: hole positions, sprocket pitch and the reading offset differ.

5. Single bed vs double bed

Single bed — one needle bed. Transfers move a loop to an adjacent needle on the same bed, only in the carriage's travel direction. Punchcard lace on Brother and Silver Reed is single-bed, and so is everything this guide and KNITCAT's decompiler schedule.

Double bed — a front and a back bed face each other. Loops move between beds (ribbing setting, links and holds, full needle and plating rib) which gives loop holding, fisherman's rib, tuck-and-rib textures and genuinely two-sided fabric. Because the destination bed is a different physical place, the sideways-direction constraint that dominates single-bed lace does not apply in the same way.

KNITCAT's Passap profile exists because Passap cards are 40 stitches wide on a different pitch — you get correct geometry, hole sizes and printable or CNC-cuttable output, but the transfer plan it prints is modelled single-bed. If you need true inter-bed action, use the machine's own pattern system for that part. The feasibility advisor says so explicitly whenever a two-bed profile is selected.

6. Float limits by gauge

A float is yarn carried across the back without being knitted. In Fair Isle it runs behind the pattern; in slip stitch it is the slipped run. Too long and it snags on needle latches, shows through the face, and lace-puckers the fabric narrower than its cast-on.

Fine3.0 mmup to ~9 stsShort bridges are cheap at fine gauge

Ways to catch a float: knit a stitch in the same colour inside the run, mirror the motif so both colours travel, or in two-colour work plan motifs at least one stitch apart. KNITCAT's advisor measures runs against the selected profile and can insert catches for you.

7. Punchcard mechanics: repeat, offset, blank rows

  • 24-stitch repeat — the card loops every 24 holes, so the pattern repeat must divide 24 evenly (24, 12, 8, 6, 4, 3, 2, 1). A 10-stitch motif will never line up.
  • Reading offset — the sensor drum reads the card several rows behind the needles (7 on Brother, 5 on Silver Reed). A correction made now shows up several rows later; when you rescue a piece mid-knit you are editing the future, not the present.
  • Blank rows are instructions — an entirely unpunched row means “knit plain”. Deleting them to save tape changes the fabric.
  • Joining cards — overlap the tape by one row's pitch and punch across the seam, or the drum loses registration at every join.
  • Direction — cards are read bottom-up in knit order, and the row nearest the tension dial... which is why printed ribbons are labelled with row numbers.

8. Designing a repeat that survives the machine

  1. Pick the machine profile first: it decides gauge, pitch, hole size and carriage rules.
  2. Draw the repeat so the width divides 24 (or 40 on Passap). Use symmetry mirrors to check tiling.
  3. Place decreases where you want the slant, then place eyelets so every hole is paid for.
  4. Count: yarnovers minus transfers should be zero across the repeat.
  5. Run the feasibility check — it flags collisions, floats and over-tall cards with the reason.
  6. Read the carriage pass schedule and sanity-check the pass count against your patience.
  7. Preview the yarn simulation: eyelets that look tidy in 3D usually behave at the machine.
  8. Export at 1:1, verify the calibration ruler, then punch or cut.

9. FAQ

What is the difference between an eyelet and a transfer on a knitting machine?
Both leave a hole. A transfer physically slides an existing loop onto the neighbouring needle, emptying one needle, creating a left- or right-leaning slant and reducing the stitch count by one. An eyelet (yarnover) is the hole itself, produced by emptying a needle and then knitting a plain row so yarn bridges the gap. A yarnover adds openwork without directional shaping; a transfer adds openwork plus shaping — which is why lace repeats pair them to keep the stitch count constant.
Why does a Brother lace carriage need plain knit rows after lace passes?
The lace carriage selects and transfers but feeds no yarn. After the transfer passes the empty needles must be fed loops by the main knit carriage, which is why two plain knit rows follow each lace sequence — and why the punchcard has to contain those blank rows.
Can two adjacent stitches transfer in the same carriage pass?
Not always. A loop moving into a needle that is simultaneously vacating is a read-write race that drops stitches; cascading transfers (i → i+1 and i+1 → i+2) and two loops landing on one needle conflict as well. A scheduler must split them across passes, alternating carriage direction.
What is the difference between single bed and double bed knitting machines?
A single-bed machine has one needle bed, so a transfer moves a loop to an adjacent needle on the same bed and only in the carriage's direction of travel. A double-bed machine has front and back beds, so loops move between beds — loop holding, fisherman's rib, tuck-and-rib, circular knitting — and transfers are not bound to a sideways neighbour. Punchcard lace on Brother and Silver Reed is a single-bed technique.
How long can a float be on a knitting machine?
Roughly seven stitches at standard 4.5 mm gauge and five at bulky 9 mm. Longer floats snag on needle latches and pucker the face. Catch them by knitting a stitch in the same colour inside the run, mirroring the motif, or planning two-colour motifs at least a stitch apart.
Why is my punchcard read several rows below the needles?
The card is gripped by a feed belt and read by a sensor drum behind the working needles, so the row being read is not the row being knitted: about seven rows on Brother standard gauge, five on Silver Reed. Corrections take effect that many rows later.
