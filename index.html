Eyelets vs transfers — two ways to make the same hole

They both leave a hole, so it looks like one is redundant. It isn't. On a machine they are the two halves of every lace repeat — the feeder and the decreaser — and a fabric that uses only one of them either grows or shrinks.

Why a hole needs a transfer in the first place

A knitting machine cannot lay a yarn round an empty needle on command. To get an eyelet you must first empty a needle, then knit past it so the yarn bridges the gap and loops up as the hole. That is exactly what the decompiler does with the ○ Eyelet cell: it schedules a one-needle transfer to vacate that needle (see js/compiler/lace-decompiler.js), and the following plain knit row catches the yarnover. On a chart an eyelet is therefore an intent — “a hole here” — and the transfer is the mechanism that pays for it.

What each lace cell actually costs the fabric
Chart cell Carriage action Stitch count Look in the cloth
○ Eyelet (yarnover) Compiles to a one-needle transfer that empties the needle; next plain row wraps yarn round it. Neutral on its own — it opens a hole without eating a loop. Round, even hole. No direction, no shaping.
↖ / ↗ Transfer Slides an existing loop onto the neighbouring needle on the same bed, in the carriage's travel direction. −1 needle in work. A hole that leans: the slant is the shaping.
∧ Double dec Two transfers onto the middle needle, split across a L→R and a R→L pass. 3 → 1. Centred eyelet with two spokes.
| Plain knit K-carriage only. The anchor rows Brother's lace carriage cannot knit for you. Unchanged. Stockinette; locks each eyelet at its settled size.

So when do I reach for which?

  • Hole, no shaping → eyelet. Fan lace, eyelet rows, scalloped shells, anything that should stay the same width.
  • Hole and a line that leans → transfer. It pulls the fabric off to one side, which is how a leaf vein or a shaped edge is built.
  • A repeat that must keep its needle count → pair them: one yarnover per one transfer (or two per centred double decrease). Balance them across the repeat or the piece flares at the selvedge.
  • Both in the same row → absolutely normal, and it is what real machine lace is. The decompiler will still split them across passes whenever two transfers would collide.

Single bed vs double bed — why this tool says “same bed”

KNITCAT schedules single-bed machines: Brother KH-830 / KH-881 / KH-911 with an LC-2, Silver Reed SK-280 / Studio / Singer, Toyota. There is one needle bed, so a transfer can only move a loop to an adjacent needle on that same bed, and the cam can only do it while the carriage travels in that direction. That single constraint is what makes machine lace a scheduling problem — hence the DAG, the collision detection, and the plain anchor rows.

A double-bed machine (Passap Duo 80 / E6000, or a Brother / Silver Reed fitted with a ribber such as the KR-850 / SR-140) has a front and a back bed, so loops are moved between beds: holding loops, fisherman's rib, tuck-and-rib, circular knitting, and transfers that are not bound to a sideways neighbour. The Passap profile here still gives you correct 40-stitch card geometry, pitch and hole sizes, but its printed transfer plan is modelled as single-bed. True inter-bed transfers need that machine's own pattern system (or a hand-written P-Card). The Feasibility check flags the card whenever you are pointed at a two-bed profile.
